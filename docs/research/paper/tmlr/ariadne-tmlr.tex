\begin{abstract}
Almost every question-answering system over knowledge graphs places a language
model between the question and the database: the model writes a query, the query
runs, and an answer is composed from the result. That design makes a class of
controlled comparisons ill-posed, because a stochastic query-writing step sits
between every manipulation and every score.

We describe a system that removes it. Ariadne answers natural-language security
questions over two live knowledge graphs that are never merged and that speak
different query languages -- MITRE ATT\&CK in Neo4j (Cypher) and MITRE CAPEC in
Apache TinkerPop (Gremlin) -- and on every shipped path \emph{no language model
writes a database query}. We use the resulting determinism as an instrument.

Our primary methodological finding concerns the instrument used to score such
systems at all. Grading models have a \emph{resolution floor}, and near the
ceiling most cannot represent the effect sizes this literature reports. Of three
local graders scored on answers of known quality, one emitted a single distinct
value in the near-ceiling band, one emitted two, and one of those two
\emph{inverted} the ordering of mediocre and good answers in exactly the score
range where our systems sit. Correlation against human labels -- the statistic
the field reports -- cannot detect this.

We then run four controlled comparisons the architecture makes possible, three of
which are null or negative. Hand-written query templates do not hold the system's
accuracy: a schema-derived, template-free path matches them on the schema they
were written for and exceeds them by $+0.1684$ judged correctness on $288$
held-out questions. Templates nevertheless beat a model writing the queries
($+0.0822$), a result that is positive under both grader families and
statistically solid under only one -- and we can say \emph{which} one only
because the instrument was characterised first. Federating across two engines
buys no accuracy over merging ($-0.0334$ and $+0.0000$), though building the
merged store required rewriting $1{,}083$ of $9{,}138$ nodes while federation
rewrote none. Finally, benchmarks co-authored with the system under test
overstate it.

We report what the design costs: exact-record identity falls from $1.0000$ to
$0.0000$ across five paraphrase tiers. We release the full experimental record,
including six experiments retained because an earlier measurement of ours was
wrong.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The architecture in this paper is not the contribution, and saying so early is
what makes the rest defensible. Mediator--wrapper federation is twenty-five years
old. Templates rather than free generation is a named family in knowledge-base
question answering. Attributing a failure to its earliest failing stage is
published prior art, as is read-only enforcement on generated graph
queries.\claimref{D-027} A reviewer who suspects we claim any of these is right
to reject.

What is unoccupied is the \emph{measurement}. Consider the question: do
hand-written query templates carry a graph-QA system's accuracy? Answering it
requires two systems identical everywhere except in how the query is produced. In
an architecture where a model writes the query you cannot build the second: the
stochastic step remains between the manipulation and the score. The comparison is
not difficult, it is ill-posed. A system that generates no query with a model is
therefore a different \emph{instrument}, not merely a different implementation.

Using that instrument taught us something we did not set out to find, and which
we now believe is the more portable result. Every judged number in this
literature, ours included, is produced by a language model scoring another
model's output. The field validates those graders by correlation against human
labels. Correlation is the wrong statistic. A grader can correlate well overall
and still be unable to represent a $0.03$ difference in the band where a paper's
headline sits; worse, it can \emph{reverse} the ordering inside a band while
leaving the global correlation intact. We measured both failures in local graders
we had been using, and we found them only because a near-miss forced the check.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A calibration procedure for LLM graders that reports \emph{resolution} and
      \emph{inversion} rather than correlation, and the finding that two of three
      local graders cannot adjudicate the effect sizes we intended to publish
      (\S\ref{sec:grader}).\expref{EXP-2026-09-008}
\item A federated graph-QA system dispatching native queries in two graph query
      languages to two live, independently governed stores, with no model-written
      query on any shipped path (\S\ref{sec:system}).\claimref{C-007}
\item Four controlled comparisons that architecture makes possible, three of them
      null or negative (\S\ref{sec:templates}--\S\ref{sec:coauthor}).
\item An accounting of what the design costs off its own vocabulary
      (\S\ref{sec:cliff}), and of two scoring faults we deliberately did not fix
      (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).
\end{enumerate}

We claim priority on implementing and measuring. Nothing more.

\paragraph{Relation to TMLR's criteria.} This submission contains no
state-of-the-art result and does not claim architectural novelty. Three of its
four system-level findings are null or negative. We submit it because its claims
are, we believe, supported by the evidence presented, and because the grader
result in \S\ref{sec:grader} bears on how anyone in this audience validates an
LLM-judged number.

\section{System}
\label{sec:system}

Five stages: \textbf{plan $\rightarrow$ route $\rightarrow$ retrieve
$\rightarrow$ verify $\rightarrow$ compose}. A planner decomposes a question into
sub-questions and assigns each to a source. Per-source specialists emit a native
query in that source's own language -- Cypher against Neo4j for ATT\&CK, Gremlin
against TinkerPop for CAPEC. Results are joined on shared identifiers over a
declared bridge. A verifier admits or rejects each candidate claim against
retrieved records. A composer writes the answer.

\paragraph{Where the model seams are.} A language model may plan; it may grade,
in evaluation only; and it may compose prose. \textbf{A language model never
writes a query that reaches a store.}\claimref{C-007} The \texttt{llm\_query} arm
of \S\ref{sec:generation} exists solely as a comparator and is not a shipped
path.

\paragraph{Which arms narrate, stated precisely.} The composition seam is where
the arms differ, and the distinction is load-bearing for
\S\ref{sec:limitations}. Each answer artifact records its own configuration:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Arm & Query path & Composition (\texttt{run\_meta.synthesis}) \\
\midrule
\texttt{deterministic} & model-free & \texttt{deterministic} — model-free \\
\texttt{generic}       & model-free & \texttt{llm (claim-grounded)} — \textbf{a model composes} \\
\texttt{llm\_query}    & a model writes the query & \texttt{llm (claim-grounded)} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

So ``no model writes a query'' is exact, and ``the system is deterministic'' is
not: \texttt{generic} — the arm carrying the primary result of
\S\ref{sec:templates} — composes its prose with a language model, and its judged
figures therefore carry a run-to-run variance we report as unmeasured in
\S\ref{sec:limitations}. Only \texttt{deterministic} is model-free end to end,
and that is verified rather than asserted: two full runs over the 284-question
population are byte-identical in every field the scorer reads, differing only in
wall-clock duration.\expref{EXP-2026-09-010}

\paragraph{The verifier, ablated.} Each of the verifier's four claim gates was
disabled individually over $888$ candidate claims. Disabling any single gate
admitted \emph{zero} additional unsupported claims.\expref{EXP-2026-08-014} We
report this as a weaker result than it sounds: on this population the gates are
mutually redundant, not individually load-bearing.\claimref{C-017}

\paragraph{Stores.} The CAPEC side loads $6{,}374$ vertices and $8{,}582$ edges
across $25$ node types and $34$ edge types.\expref{EXP-2026-09-001}

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Arms.} Three, differing only in query production.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Arm & Query produced by \\
\midrule
\texttt{deterministic} & Hand-written parameterised templates \\
\texttt{generic}       & Assembly from the graph schema at runtime; no templates \\
\texttt{llm\_query}    & A language model, given schema, resolved seeds, gate constraints, repair loop \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Question populations.} Nine, of which four carry weight here: a
development catalogue; an expansion bank built from a schema census so coverage
is structural rather than convenient ($572$
questions)\expref{EXP-2026-09-001}; a $288$-question holdout split off and sealed
by SHA-256 before any repair was written\expref{EXP-2026-09-007}; and $182$
questions taken verbatim from two published external question sets, neither
authored with sight of this code.\expref{EXP-2026-08-010}

\paragraph{Grading.} Every judged figure was produced under \emph{two named
grader families}, each identified by exact model id, quantisation and date --
never by relay alias. This is not fastidiousness. One alias in this project
served three different models over its lifetime, and a figure attributed to an
alias is not attributable at all.\claimref{D-028}

\paragraph{Statistics.} Paired percentile bootstrap, $2{,}000$ resamples, seed
$1729$, $95\%$ intervals throughout.

\paragraph{Two scoring faults we did not fix.} Abstention boilerplate credits a
refusal, and the scorer recognises the source's identifier vocabulary rather than
the graph's ($0.185$ on the subset where it was quantified). \textbf{Both inflate
scores.} Fixing either partway through a comparison would have invalidated the
comparison, so both are priced into every figure below and restated in
\S\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Grading models have a resolution floor}
\label{sec:grader}

\paragraph{Why correlation is the wrong statistic.} A grader is an instrument. An
instrument is characterised by what it can \emph{resolve}, not by how well it
agrees on average. We therefore ask a narrower question than agreement: holding
prompt and protocol fixed, on answers whose true quality band is known by
construction, \emph{how many distinct values does this grader emit in that band,
and can it therefore represent the effect we intend to publish?}

The acceptance criterion was pre-registered before any judgment existed: a grader
is usable near the ceiling iff $\text{fraction at }1.0 < 0.95$ and
$\text{distinct values} > 2$.\expref{EXP-2026-09-008}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Near-ceiling resolution, $n=20$ answers per grader.\expref{EXP-2026-09-008}}
\begin{tabular}{lrrlc}
\toprule
Grader & Mean & Distinct & Values emitted & Usable \\
\midrule
\texttt{gemma4-31B-mtp} & 1.0000 & \textbf{1} & $\{1.0\}$ & no \\
\texttt{gemma-4-26B-it} & 0.9750 & \textbf{2} & $\{0.5, 1.0\}$ & no \\
\texttt{qwen38-27B-v2}  & 0.9800 & 3 & $\{0.8, 0.9, 1.0\}$ & \textbf{yes} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

A grader emitting one value in a band reports a contrast of exactly zero in that
band \emph{whatever is true}. This is not sampling noise; more samples do not
help. It is a floor, and it rises as answers approach the ceiling.

\paragraph{Inversion, which is worse.} \texttt{gemma-4-26B-it} does not merely
lose resolution. Scored across constructed bands it places the \emph{low} band at
$0.6435$ above the \emph{mid} band at $0.6239$, when the constructed ordering is
mid above low.\expref{EXP-2026-09-008} That is precisely the $0.40$--$0.65$ range
in which all three of our arms sit on the holdout. A grader that adds noise is
survivable. A grader that reverses an ordering is a different failure, and it is
\emph{invisible to the correlation statistics the field reports}. We halted a
planned third-family grading pass on this evidence, with zero judgments written.

\paragraph{What this cost us, reported as a limitation.} Our co-authorship-bias
effect is $0.0322$ under the one grader that can resolve it and $0.0000$ under a
grader that cannot.\expref{EXP-2026-09-006}\claimref{C-021} The second number
reads as a refutation and is an instrument limit. Six of twelve cells in our
cross-family re-grade are saturated under the second family. Separately, a
tie-handling defect in our roll-up -- excluding pairs tied by the reference
grader but not pairs tied by the candidate -- was one revision away from
publishing \emph{four arm-order reversals that do not exist}. The correct count
is zero.\expref{EXP-2026-09-006}

\paragraph{What it bought us.} Because the instrument was characterised, we can
state which of the two grader families in \S\ref{sec:generation} to believe,
rather than averaging them. That is the entire argument for doing this.

\section{Templates versus schema-derived construction}
\label{sec:templates}

This result replaces a claim our own experiments refuted: we previously held that
the hand-written templates were where the accuracy lived.\claimref{C-003}

On the catalogue the templates were written for, the two paths are
indistinguishable. Across ten anchor-disjoint banks, templates score
$0.99 \pm 0.0225$ and the schema-derived path $1.00 \pm 0.0000$; paired
difference $-0.01 \pm 0.0225$.\expref{EXP-2026-08-015} Templates do not hold the
accuracy; on home ground they merely match it.

On a schema nobody wrote templates for, the ordering does not narrow -- it
inverts. Over the same ten banks on an identity schema: templates
$0.2392 \pm 0.0674$, schema-derived $0.8514 \pm 0.0890$, paired difference
$-0.6121 \pm 0.0981$.\expref{EXP-2026-08-015} On a blind set over that schema,
retrieval is $0.1964$ for templates against $0.7857$
derived.\expref{EXP-2026-08-011}

\paragraph{The failure mode matters more than the gap.} On the foreign schema the
template arm did not decline. It asserted: unsupported-claim rate $0.8214$
against the derived path's $0.1071$.\expref{EXP-2026-08-011} A template that does
not fit the schema still fires, still retrieves, and still answers
confidently.\claimref{C-023}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Judged correctness, schema-derived minus templates, on unseen questions.}
\begin{tabular}{llrr}

Population & $n$ & Gap & Source \\

\paragraph{The sharpest single piece of evidence in the paper.} We diagnosed and
repaired two retrieval defects in the template arm. Template retrieval rose by
$+0.0684$. Template judged correctness moved
$+0.0012$.\expref{EXP-2026-09-005} Retrieving the right records did not make the
answers right. Whatever the templates cost, it was not recall.

\paragraph{A weighting caveat we insist on.} The census-weighted repair gain is
$+0.0017$, against $+0.0503$ pooled.\expref{EXP-2026-09-007} The pooled figure is
an artifact of a bank that deliberately over-samples rare question shapes. Both
must be quoted, in that order.

\section{Templates versus a model writing the queries}
\label{sec:generation}

This is the weakest of our three system comparisons and we do not lead with it.

Given the real schema, resolved seed entities, the gate's constraints and a
repair loop, a model writing the queries scores $0.4039$ judged correctness on
the holdout, against templates at $0.4862$ and the schema-derived path at

\section{Federated versus merged execution}
\label{sec:federation}

We built both conditions, changed one variable, and used no model in either arm.
Merged minus federated judged correctness: $-0.0334$ on one blind bank,
$+0.0000$ on a second; mean $-0.0167$.\expref{EXP-2026-08-009} Federating bought
no accuracy. We state that as a result rather than working around
it.\claimref{C-015}

What survives the null is structural and countable. Constructing the merged store
required rewriting $1{,}083$ of $9{,}138$ nodes -- $22.71\%$ of the CAPEC side,
$11.85\%$ of the whole store -- including re-prefixing $189$ taxonomy entries.
Federation rewrote zero.\expref{EXP-2026-08-017} Merging is not free; it is a
data-editing cost paid once per source and again on every refresh.\claimref{C-022}

\paragraph{A claim we deliberately do not make.} A third graph was loaded
unmodified beside both catalogues. It is tempting to say it could not legally
have been copied into a merged store. Our own record forbids that claim: the
contractual argument is a motivation, the identity records are synthetic, and
what was demonstrated is narrower -- keeping the sources separate asked that
graph for nothing.

\paragraph{An apparent contradiction we owe the reader.} SPLIT-RAG reports a
$17.6$ Hit-point gap between split and merged conditions where ours is neutral.
The most likely reconciliation is that their ablation varies agent count
alongside store topology, making it a different variable. We state this
ourselves rather than leaving it for a reviewer.

\section{Co-authored benchmarks overstate their systems}
\label{sec:coauthor}

Evaluating a system on questions written alongside the code that answers them
inflates the result. Measured on the same system, same grader, same session: the
gap is $0.1472$ before a composer fix\expref{EXP-2026-08-007} and $0.0483$
after,\expref{EXP-2026-08-008} and $0.0322$ under the named, resolution-checked
grader.\expref{EXP-2026-09-006}\claimref{C-021}

The bias's dominant form is not a wrong answer. It is a question the bank does
not contain. This is why \S\ref{sec:related}'s external question sets matter more
than their size suggests.

\section{Run-to-run variance, where it lives and what it costs}
\label{sec:variance}

Every judged figure in this paper is one run of one arm. We measured whether that
matters, separately for each seam.

\paragraph{The query path reproduces exactly.} Two independent runs of both
model-free arms over the same 284 questions return byte-identical retrieval,
coverage, claims and evidence.\expref{EXP-2026-09-010} This is the first direct
measurement of the determinism the architecture is supposed to buy, and it holds.

\paragraph{The composition seam does not.} \texttt{generic} narrates with a
language model, and 143 of 276 comparable answers are worded differently on a
rerun at the same seed.\expref{EXP-2026-09-010} \texttt{deterministic}, which
composes without a model, is identical on all 284.

\paragraph{Seed choice barely matters for the query-writing arm.} Across three
seeded runs, the across-seed spread of \texttt{llm\_query} is $0.0089$, an order
of magnitude below the $0.0822$ margin of
\S\ref{sec:generation}.\expref{EXP-2026-09-009}

\paragraph{But the wording variation does not reach the score.} Across
\textbf{six} seeded runs of \texttt{generic}, the balanced across-seed standard
deviation of judged correctness is $\mathbf{0.0074}$, against the $0.1623$ gap it
supports on the same population --- a ratio of $7.3$ to
$1$.\expref{EXP-2026-09-011}

We report six because three were not enough to say how small the spread is. The
first three seeds gave $0.0039$; three further seeds, pre-committed before they
were graded, gave $0.0097$, and the pooled figure is $0.0074$ ---
\textbf{nearly double the three-seed estimate}. The means of the two sets differ
by $0.0014$, so this is dispersion being sampled better rather than two
populations. \textbf{Quote $0.0074$.}

\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Both facts hold at once and the reconciliation is the architecture. Retrieval,
coverage, claims and evidence are byte-identical across runs; the verifier
admits claims against those fixed records; so what the narrator is free to vary
is \emph{phrasing}, not \emph{content}. A grader scoring what was asserted sees
the same assertions in different words.

This is a stronger result than determinism would have been. A system whose prose
is fixed says nothing about whether its scores are robust to prose. This one
re-words the majority of its answers and moves $0.004$.

\paragraph{An instrument property, measured three times.} The two grading blocks
are the same grader over the same answers, differing only in when they ran:
block 1 means $0.6522$, block 2 means $0.6411$ --- $-0.0111$ for being graded
second.\expref{EXP-2026-09-011} Two earlier measurements agree in size and
direction.\expref{EXP-2026-08-022}\expref{EXP-2026-09-009} We counterbalance
because of it: ungraded in balanced order, that block effect alone is nearly
three times the entire across-seed signal, and would be reported as seed
variance.

\paragraph{What is not claimed.} Six seeds give a better-sampled spread, not a
distribution, and the correction from three to six moved it in the direction of
\emph{more} variance rather than less. The two sets were graded in separate
sessions, so a session effect cannot be separated from seed variance here.
Both blocks are the same grader, so this is position-balanced \emph{repeatability},
not cross-grader agreement --- and \S\ref{sec:grader} shows the other family can
invert an ordering on a population like this one, so cross-grader stability
must not be inferred from it.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Every item here is measured. Stating them is cheaper than being caught.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The templates-over-generation result is grader-dependent}
      (\S\ref{sec:generation}): solid under one family on both halves, not solid
      under the other on either.
\item \textbf{No external system baseline.} Every comparison is between arms of
      this system. We have $182$ externally-authored \emph{questions} -- an
      outside anchor on the inputs, not on the systems. This is the single
      largest gap and we do not minimise it.
\item \textbf{No human evaluation.} Every correctness number is a model grading a
      model. \S\ref{sec:grader} characterises that instrument; it does not
      substitute for a human anchor.
\item \textbf{The holdout is a random split of a bank we authored.} It controls
      for tuning to particular questions. It does \emph{not} control for the
      co-authorship bias we ourselves measure in \S\ref{sec:coauthor}.
\item \textbf{Several experiments were run once}, with seeds unrecorded, and this
      applies to the arm carrying the primary result. \texttt{deterministic} is
      exempt by measurement: it is byte-identical across two full runs in every
      scored field, so a single run of it is sufficient by
      construction.\expref{EXP-2026-09-010} \textbf{\texttt{generic} is not
      exempt, and this is measured rather than suspected}: on a rerun at the same
      seed, against the same store, \textbf{143 of 276 comparable answers are
      worded differently}.\expref{EXP-2026-09-010} Its figures in
      \S\ref{sec:templates} are therefore one wording of that arm, not a point
      estimate of it.

      Two things bound it, and both are now measured. First, the variation is
      confined to \emph{one field}: every retrieval, coverage, claim and evidence
      field is byte-identical across runs, so what varies is the prose and not
      what the system found. Second, and decisively, \textbf{the variation does
      not reach the score}: the balanced across-seed standard deviation over six
      seeds is $0.0074$ against a $0.1623$ effect.\expref{EXP-2026-09-011} The figures in
      \S\ref{sec:templates} may therefore be read as point estimates. The
      wording instability remains reported, because it is true and because a
      reader who reruns the system will see it.

\item \textbf{The query-writing arm is stable across seeds, so its result is not
      a single-draw artifact}: three seeded runs give an across-seed spread of
      $0.0089$ against a margin of $0.0822$.\expref{EXP-2026-09-009} We record
      this because the natural objection to \S\ref{sec:generation} — that one
      draw established nothing — is one we tested and can answer.
\item \textbf{Two scoring faults left unfixed by design} (\S\ref{sec:design}).
      Both inflate scores.
\item \textbf{Weighting matters}: the census-weighted repair gain is $+0.0017$
      against $+0.0503$ pooled.
\item \textbf{One domain, two schemas, two query languages.} Nothing here is a
      law about query construction until it replicates where we wrote no
      templates.
\item \textbf{Source coverage is partial}: $12$ of $34$ edge types reached, and
      $0$ of $12$ facet properties used at census
      time.\expref{EXP-2026-09-001}
\item \textbf{A reserved-word Gremlin binding} costs the model-written-query arm
      evidence on some questions. Pre-existing and priced into the figures, but
      whether it originates with us or with the model is undiagnosed.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Broader Impact Statement}

The system answers questions over public cybersecurity taxonomies. Two impacts
are worth stating.

First, \S\ref{sec:cliff} shows a retrieval-grounded, citation-producing system
answering confidently about entities that do not exist, with hundreds of
genuinely retrieved and genuinely cited records behind the answer. Citations are
routinely read as evidence of grounding. In a security setting, a fluent, cited
answer about a technique that was never published is a plausible route to a bad
operational decision. We report the failure rather than tuning it out of the
evaluation, and we regard the ability to decline with a machine-checkable proof
as the more important property of the design.

Second, \S\ref{sec:grader} implies that some published differences between
LLM-judged systems may be below the resolution of the judge that measured them.
We make no claim about any specific published result. We note only that
resolution is not currently reported, that correlation does not reveal it, and
that we found two graders in our own pipeline that could not adjudicate the
effect we intended to publish.
